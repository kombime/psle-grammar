Where Do You Breathe?

In a song sheet, a singer looks for a tiny mark shaped like a comma. It's called a breath mark, and it tells them where to pause so the words make sense. A comma in a long sentence does the same job for your reader. Leave it out, and your reader runs two parts together and gets the wrong idea.

The three breath marks

Two words you'll see all through this lesson:

The singer is the person or thing doing the action (the subject): I, we, Mum, the bus, my group.

The breath mark is the comma. In a long sentence, there are three places where you'll usually need one.

  1. After the intro. If the sentence starts with an opening part before the singer appears, breathe when the intro ends.

    As soon as the bell rang, we rushed to the canteen.

  2. Before a new verse. If and, but, so, or, yet brings in a new singer with their own action, breathe just before that word.

    We spent more than $30, so the cashier gave us a voucher.

  3. Before an echo. If the sentence ends with an -ing part that adds an extra action or a result, breathe before it.

    I groaned, crumpling my wrinkled origami paper.

And where you don't breathe

Never put a comma between the singer and their verb: My best friend Lila, folded a swan. My best friend Lila folded a swan.

A comma doesn't belong just anywhere a sentence feels long. Only add one when one of the three breath marks applies.

Three traps

These come straight from real compositions. The first one is by far the most common.

Trap 1: Running straight through the intro

As soon as Mrs Lin saw what was happening she stopped the older boy.

As soon as Mrs Lin saw what was happening, she stopped the older boy.

Without the breath, the reader runs into "happening she" and has to go back. The longer the intro, the more your reader needs that comma. The same goes for short opening words like Suddenly, Luckily and At first.

Trap 2: Breathing on the wrong side of "so"

We were very hungry so, we ordered two plates of chicken rice.

We were very hungry, so we ordered two plates of chicken rice.

The breath comes before the joining word, never after it. "so we ordered…" is the new verse, and it has to stay together. The same goes for and and but.

Trap 3: Breathing when there's no new verse

I grabbed my umbrella, and ran to the bus stop.

I grabbed my umbrella and ran to the bus stop.

We stayed indoors, because it was raining heavily.

We stayed indoors because it was raining heavily.

In the first, there is only one singer ("I") doing two actions, so no new verse starts. In the second, the linking part comes at the end, so it isn't an intro. Neither one needs a breath.

The three breath marks up close

Each breath mark has a few common shapes. Learn to spot them.

Breath mark 1

After the intro

  • opening words
  • time and place phrases
  • linking-word parts
  • -ing openers

Opening words: Suddenly, a ring from the doorbell startled me. / Luckily, Dad had recorded my performance.

Time and place phrases: During recess the next day, Arjun showed me his card trick. / From that day on, I practised every evening.

Linking-word parts (when, as soon as, once, after, before, if, although, because): When I finished my homework, I switched on the iPad. / Although she was nervous, Nurul sang the solo beautifully.

-ing openers: Hearing the news, my brother wiped away his tears. / Seeing me struggle, Mum sat down beside me.

Breath mark 2

Before a new verse

  • , and
  • , but
  • , so
  • , or
  • , yet

Wei Ling wanted to join the race, but she had hurt her ankle.

My father played our favourite songs, and my brother sang along loudly.

Dinner was ready, so we sat down at the table.

Check: does a new singer appear after the joining word? If yes, breathe before it. (You met these joining words in the Train Carriage lesson. Here, the focus is on where the comma sits.)

Breath mark 3

Before an echo

  • an extra action
  • a result

An extra action: Lila sat beside me, folding the paper step by step. / Mum hugged me tightly, whispering that it was all right.

A result: The bus braked suddenly, throwing us all forward. / The glass vase fell off the shelf, shattering into tiny pieces.

The echo tells you what else happened at the same time, or what happened because of the main action.

Three more things the quiz will check

These aren't one of the three breath marks, but they all turn up in long sentences.

A side note needs two breaths

When you slip extra information into the middle of a sentence, such as who someone is or what something is, put a comma on both sides of it.

Our waiter, Mr Evan Sim took our orders.

Our waiter, Mr Evan Sim, took our orders.

Ms Goh, my form teacher, announced the news.

Test: take the side note out. "Our waiter took our orders" still works, so the side note needs commas on both sides.

An -ing part that describes who you saw

An -ing part that tells you what the person you just named was doing isn't an echo. It belongs to that person, so don't breathe before it.

I saw a Primary Six boy pushing a younger student.

I found my little brother hiding under the bed.

Who was pushing? The boy, not "I". So the -ing part stays attached to "the boy", with no comma.

A list of three or more

Separate each item or action with a comma. In Singapore schools, you don't usually need a comma before the final "and".

At DisneySea, we rode rides played games and watched a parade.

At DisneySea, we rode rides, played games and watched a parade.

Mark the breaths

Some questions ask if a sentence needs a comma. Others ask you to pick the version with the commas in the right places. You'll see straight away if you're right, and why. The questions get trickier as you go.

Pin This Up

  • After the intro: an opening word, phrase or linking part, then a comma, then the singer.
  • Before a new verse: a comma before and / but / so when a new singer follows.
  • Before an echo: a comma before an -ing part that adds an extra action or result.
  • Side notes get two commas, one on each side.
  • No breath: between the singer and the verb, after so, for one singer doing two actions, or before a linking part at the end.
