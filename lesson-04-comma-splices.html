A Comma Can't Pull a Carriage

Every complete sentence is a train carriage with its own engine. When you join two carriages, you need a proper coupler. A comma on its own is just a piece of string, and the second carriage rolls away. That broken join is called a comma splice.

Comma splice! "The bell rang, we ran to class." Two engines, held by a piece of string.

The three-step coupling check

Use this whenever you put a comma in the middle of a long sentence.

  1. Look for engines. An engine is a subject plus its verb that could stand alone as a full sentence.

    The bell rang → engine. we ran to class → engine.

  2. Count them. One engine? No problem. Two engines? They must be coupled properly.

    Watch for a new subject after the comma: I, he, she, it, we, they, there, or a name.

  3. Use a real coupler. Split them with a full stop, add a joining word after the comma, or use a linking word like because or when. A comma alone is never enough.

    The bell rang, so we ran to class.

The "stand alone" test

Not sure if a part is an engine? Read it out loud on its own. If it makes a full sentence, it's an engine.

"we ran to class" → a full sentence. Engine.

"when the bell rang" → you're left waiting for more. Not an engine on its own.

Three traps on the line

These show up in almost every composition. Learn to spot them in your own writing.

Trap 1: The ideas feel connected

The water was cold, it made me shiver.

The water was cold. It made me shiver.

The water was cold, so it made me shiver.

Because the ideas go together, a comma feels right. But "it made me shiver" has its own engine. The giveaway is a new subject (it, he, she, they, there…) straight after the comma.

Trap 2: "Then" isn't a coupler

We ate dinner, then we watched TV.

We ate dinner, and then we watched TV.

We ate dinner. Then we watched TV.

"Then" tells you when, but it can't join two engines. Add "and" in front of it, or start a new sentence.

Trap 3: "However" isn't a coupler either

It was raining, however we went to the beach.

It was raining. However, we went to the beach.

It was raining, but we went to the beach.

"However" and "but" mean the same thing, but they work differently. "But" is a coupler. "However" is not, so it needs a full stop before it and a comma after it. The same goes for therefore, also and besides.

Three proper couplers, and commas that are fine

Any of the first three fixes a comma splice. Pick the one that best shows how the ideas connect.

FIX 1

Full stop and a capital letter

Make two separate trains. The simplest fix, and always correct.

Nurul loves drawing. She wants to be an artist.

The library was quiet. Everyone was reading.

FIX 2

Comma + joining word

  • and
  • but
  • so
  • or
  • yet

The joining word is the coupler. The comma goes before it, never after it.

I wanted to play outside, but it was too hot.

Arjun was hungry, so he ate two plates of chicken rice.

FIX 3

A linking word

  • because
  • when
  • although
  • if
  • before
  • after
  • while
  • until

A linking word turns one carriage into a trailer that can't run alone. If the linking part comes first, put a comma after it. If it comes in the middle, you usually don't need a comma.

When the bell rang, we ran to class.

Grandma took a nap because she was tired.

NOT A SPLICE

Commas that are perfectly fine

Don't panic about every comma. These have only one engine, or a proper coupler already.

After a linking part: Although it was raining, the match went on.

In a list: We bought snacks, drinks and balloons.

After Yes, No or Oh: Yes, I will help you.

One subject, two actions: I packed my bag and waited for the bus. ("I" is the only engine.)

Two more places splices hide

The quiz includes these. They follow the same rule, but they're easy to miss.

Inside speech marks

Speech follows the same rule. Two engines inside quotation marks still need a full stop between them, just like in the Speech Bubble lesson.

"The bus is late, we will miss the show," Dad sighed.

"The bus is late. We will miss the show," Dad sighed.

No comma at all

Two engines with nothing between them are just as broken. This is called a run-on sentence. Fix it the same way.

The movie ended we all clapped.

The movie ended, and we all clapped.
