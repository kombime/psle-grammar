Zoom In,
Zoom Out

Every noun is a photo, and you're the one holding the camera. Zoom in until the name tag is sharp, and you've found one particular person, place or thing: Ms Goh, Rosyth School, the Singapore River. That's a proper noun, and it always gets a capital. Stay zoomed out and it could be any of them: a teacher, a school, a river. That's a common noun. Then widen the lens to fit more than one in the frame, and the noun turns plural. The tricky part is that the ending doesn't always just add -s.

RECFocus check
Zoom in or out?

Two dials on the lens

Every noun answers two questions. Turn the focus dial first, then the width dial.

Dial 1: Focus

Any one of its kind, or one particular, named one?

  • Common noun: things, animals, places and types of people in general. girl, dog, music, lunch, table, doctor
  • Proper noun: the name of one specific person, organisation or place. Italy, Taj Mahal, Sunday, Middle Street
  • A proper noun always begins with a capital letter.

Dial 2: Width

One in the frame, or more than one?

  • Singular: one person or thing. a doctor, that patient
  • Plural: more than one. two doctors, many patients
  • Most nouns add -s, but many follow a different pattern. Climb the plural ladder to choose the right ending.

What counts as a name? The proper-noun ID card

Kind of nameExamples
People and titlesMs Goh, Uncle Raju, Sir Stamford Raffles, Edgar Allan Poe
OrganisationsRosyth School, the National Library Board, the Singapore Zoo
Countries and placesJapan, New Zealand, Tampines, Bedok North, the Singapore River, Lake Toba
BuildingsMarina Bay Sands, the Esplanade, Changi General Hospital, the Eiffel Tower
Historical eventsWorld War II, the Japanese Occupation
Days, months, festivalsMonday, December, Hari Raya Puasa, Chinese New Year, Racial Harmony Day
Languages and nationalitiesJapanese, Malay, a Singaporean
Titles of books, films, playsCharlotte's Web, The Lion King, Harry Potter and the Half-Blood Prince

Titles: every main word takes a capital. Small words such as a, an, the, and, of, in, on stay small, unless they're the first word: The Boy in the Striped Pyjamas.

Not on the card: seasons are common nouns, so they stay small. We went to Japan in winter.

Three blurry shots

These are the mistakes that lose marks in editing.

Trap 1: Same word, two jobs

Many words can be a plain common noun or part of a name. The capital goes on only when the word is part of the name.

Common (zoomed out)Part of a name (zoomed in) my uncleUncle Raju the hospitalChangi General Hospital a riverthe Singapore River the lake at the parkLake Toba our form teacherMs Goh my grandmaGrandma (used as her name)

My Uncle took me to the Hospital. → My uncle took me to the hospital.

A clue: after my, his, the, our, a word like uncle or grandma is usually common. When you use it in place of a name ("Grandma cooked dinner"), it's a name and takes a capital.

Trap 2: Plain -s on a word that needs more

boxs → boxes · partys → parties · knifes → knives · sheeps → sheep · childrens → children

Before adding -s, check the word's last letters against the plural ladder below. Words that are already plural (children, teeth, people) never take another -s.

Trap 3: "One of the…"

One of the doctor are able to help us. → One of the doctors is able to help us.

"One of the" picks one person from a group, so the noun after it is plural (doctors). But you are still talking about one, so the verb is singular (is, has, was). Words like many, several, a few and numbers above one also need a plural noun: many patients.

The plural ladder

Look at how the word ends, then find its step on the ladder.

  1. Most nouns: add -s

    • toy → toys
    • kite → kites
    • tiger → tigers
    • parade → parades

  2. Ends in -ss, -x, -ch, -sh: add -es

    • witness → witnesses
    • tax → taxes
    • bench → benches
    • bush → bushes
    • class → classes
    • dish → dishes

    Say it aloud: the plural gets an extra beat ("bench-iz"), and the e makes room for it.

  3. Ends in -o: add -s or -es

    Vowel before the o: always -s.

    • video → videos
    • radio → radios
    • zoo → zoos
    • kangaroo → kangaroos

    Consonant before the o: some take -s, and some take -es. Learn the -es words.

    • piano → pianos
    • photo → photos
    • potato → potatoes
    • tomato → tomatoes
    • hero → heroes
    • echo → echoes

  4. Ends in -y: check the letter before it

    Consonant + y: change y to -ies.

    • lady → ladies
    • story → stories
    • city → cities
    • candy → candies
    • baby → babies
    • party → parties

    Vowel + y: just add -s.

    • toy → toys
    • monkey → monkeys
    • holiday → holidays
    • journey → journeys

  5. Ends in -f or -fe: often -ves

    • yourself → yourselves
    • wolf → wolves
    • life → lives
    • knife → knives
    • leaf → leaves
    • half → halves
    • shelf → shelves
    • thief → thieves
    • loaf → loaves

    The keepers just add -s: roof → roofs, chief → chiefs, cliff → cliffs, giraffe → giraffes, belief → beliefs.

  6. Ends in -us: change to -i

    • syllabus → syllabi
    • focus → foci
    • cactus → cacti
    • radius → radii

    The -i is already plural, so never add -s to it: cactis. (You may also see forms like "cactuses"; the quiz uses the -i forms from your book.)

  7. Irregular: the word changes inside

    • tooth → teeth
    • foot → feet
    • goose → geese
    • mouse → mice
    • man → men
    • woman → women
    • child → children
    • person → people

    These are already plural. teeths, childrens, peoples are never right in a sentence like "The children laughed".

  8. Same in singular and plural

    • one sheep, twenty sheep
    • one deer, five deer
    • one aircraft, two aircraft
    • one species, many species

  9. Always plural: even when there's just one

    • scissors
    • spectacles
    • trousers
    • jeans
    • shorts
    • goggles
    • binoculars
    • pliers

    They come in two matching halves, so they're always plural and take a plural verb: My spectacles are on the table. Your shorts is dirty.

Special shots

The quiz checks these too.

"A pair of" decides the verb

When you count an always-plural noun with pair, the verb follows pair.

A pair of scissors is in the drawer.

This pair of trousers is too big.

Two pairs of goggles are missing.

Compound nouns: the main word goes plural

In a noun made of several words, put the plural on the main word, the person.

passer-by → passers-by · sister-in-law → sisters-in-law

brother-in-law → brothers-in-law · son-in-law → sons-in-law

passer-bys sister-in-laws

Hundred, thousand, dozen after a number

After a number, these words stay single. The noun after them is plural.

two hundred girls · five thousand runners · three dozen eggs

two hundreds girls

With no number in front, they take -s and "of": hundreds of people, dozens of children.

Spotting the proper nouns in a sentence

Count the names, not the capitals. In "Mr Tan's car broke down near the Singapore River on Tuesday", there are three proper nouns: Mr Tan, the Singapore River and Tuesday. A name of several words, like The Lion King, counts as one proper noun.

To find the common noun for a name, zoom out: Changi Airport → an airport; Japan → a country.
