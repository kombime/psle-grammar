Keep the Seesaw Level

Every sentence is a seesaw. The subject sits on one end and the verb sits on the other. One rider needs a one-rider verb; a crowd needs a crowd verb. Get it wrong and the sentence tips over.

Level. One dog, so the verb takes the -s: runs.

The three-step balance check

Use this every time you're choosing between is/are, has/have, was/were, or a verb with or without -s.

  1. Find the real subject. Ask: who or what is doing the action? Cover up any extra words that describe it.

    The bag of oranges is heavy. → The rider is the bag.

  2. Count it. Is it one person or thing, or more than one?

    One bag. One rider.

  3. Match the verb. One takes the one-rider verb: is, has, was, runs. More than one takes the crowd verb: are, have, were, run.

    The bag is heavy. The bags are heavy.

The S swap

Here's the twist that confuses everyone. On a noun, an -s usually means more than one. On a verb, an -s means just one. So the -s jumps across the seesaw. It never sits on both ends.

The bird sings.
The birds sing.

This matters in the present tense and with is/are, has/have and was/were. Most past-tense verbs, like walked, stay the same for one or many.

Three traps that tip the seesaw

These are the mistakes that show up again and again in grammar papers and compositions.

Trap 1: Matching the nearest noun

The box of crayons are on the table.

The box of crayons is on the table.

"Crayons" sits right next to the verb, so it's tempting to match it. But the box is the rider. Put your finger over the "of…" part and the answer becomes obvious. It works the other way too: The leaves on the tree are brown.

Trap 2: "Each of" and "one of"

Each of the pupils have a name tag.

Each of the pupils has a name tag.

One of my cousins plays the piano.

The plural word after "of" is a decoy. The rider is "each" or "one", and both mean a single person or thing.

Trap 3: "There is" and "There are"

There is three apples in the basket.

There are three apples in the basket.

In these sentences the subject comes after the verb, so look ahead to find the rider. The same goes for sentences starting with "Here": Here are your books. Here is your book.

Who's on the seesaw?

Some subjects are made of more than one word. Here's how to count them.

Crowd verb

Two subjects joined by "and"

"And" adds riders together, so the subject is more than one.

Wei Ling and her brother walk to school.

My pencil case and my water bottle are in my bag.

Match the nearer one

"Either… or" and "Neither… nor"

Only one choice gets on the seesaw, so the verb matches the subject closest to it.

Neither Arjun nor Nurul is late.

Either Marcus or his sisters are bringing the cake.

Neither the twins nor their mother knows the way.

One-rider verb

Each, every, everyone, nobody

These words look at people one at a time, so they always take the one-rider verb, even when they talk about a whole class.

Everyone in Primary 5 has a library card.

Nobody likes a soggy sandwich.

Even with two nouns: Every boy and girl gets a medal.

One-rider verb

Groups acting as one

Words like class, team, choir, family and committee name many people, but when the group acts together as one unit, it takes the one-rider verb. This is what PSLE expects.

Our class is going on an excursion.

The committee has made its decision.

Four tricky riders

The quiz includes these less obvious patterns. Each one still follows the three-step check once you know who the real rider is.

"As well as" and "along with"

Unlike "and", these phrases don't add a rider. They're extra words, so cover them up, like the "of…" part in Trap 1. "Together with" works the same way.

Mr Tan, as well as his pupils, is at the assembly.

The children, along with their teacher, are at the bus stop.

Pairs: scissors, spectacles, trousers

These words are always plural on their own, because they're made of two matching parts. But add "a pair of" and the rider becomes the pair: one pair.

My spectacles are on the table.

A pair of scissors is in the drawer.

"The number of" vs. "A number of"

The number of means one figure, a count, so it takes the one-rider verb. A number of means "several", a crowd, so it takes the crowd verb.

The number of pupils is growing.

A number of pupils are absent today.

Amounts of money and time

When you talk about money, time or distance as one total amount, it's one rider, even though the number is more than one.

Five dollars is too much for a pencil.

Three days is a long time to wait.

Pin This Up

  • Find the real rider. Cover up "of…", "as well as…" and "along with…".
  • One rider → is, has, was, runs. A crowd → are, have, were, run.
  • "And" makes a crowd. "Or" / "nor": match the nearer one.
  • Each, every, everyone, one of → one rider.
  • A group acting as one (class, team, committee) → one rider.
  • There / Here → look after the verb to find the rider.
